\begin{abstract}
A decision head reads a state and a list of options and returns a probability for each option in one forward pass; a caller answers when the top probability clears a threshold and escalates otherwise. Once deployed, it usually observes only whether the option it chose was right: logged bandit feedback. We adapt such a head, a LoRA adapter on Qwen3-0.6B-Base and 1.7B-Base, from that feedback, and report three results.

First, a proper score of the sampled action's own probability is not a usable REINFORCE reward. That a detached, parameter-dependent reward drops the pathwise term of the gradient is known; we give the corollary for this reward: against a group-mean baseline every wrong action earns at least the gold action's reward, the lowest-probability wrong action sampled gets the largest advantage, and at $K = 2$ the signal is exactly zero. In practice it cut one score task's accuracy from .636 to .111.

Second, with full labels, no bandit objective beat cross-entropy plus one temperature on calibration under label-space shift; the best arm was level, $-$.002 [$-$.006, +.004]. This replicates a concurrent finding under a stricter protocol: transfer to unseen label schemas, a continued cross-entropy control, and four bandit arms under one temperature.

Third, from logged bandit feedback alone, Chosen-Action Proper Scoring (CAPS), the direct method with a proper score that fits the chosen action's probability to its logged outcome by the pathwise gradient, beats fine-tuning on confirmed answers only, which is positive-unlabeled learning with the negatives discarded: on Banking77 by +2.6 points [1.9, 3.3] in accuracy, .053 in ECE and .0095 in AURC. It stays 1.5 points [1.0, 2.0] below full-label training at 0.6B and .6 points [.2, 1.1] below it at 1.7B, so the gap shrinks with scale (three CAPS seeds, one full-label seed at 1.7B), and propensity weights add nothing, as expected of a well-specified regression. Under feedback flipped at a known rate, CAPS calibrates to the noisy channel without any correction, backward correction recovers most of the clean calibration, and early stopping on the held-out log-likelihood of logged outcomes prevents the memorisation of the single noisy draw.
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}\label{sec:1}

\textbf{The decision head.} A decision head answers typed questions about a text state: yes or no, one of K options, or a level on a scale. The options are part of each request, so the same model answers label sets it was never trained on. It returns a full distribution per question from the option-letter probabilities at an answer slot, in one forward pass, with no text generated. The caller's code turns the probabilities into actions, for example by answering above a confidence threshold and escalating below it, which is selective classification with a reject option. That only works if the probabilities are calibrated and rank the errors lowest. Ours is a LoRA adapter on Qwen3-0.6B-Base and 1.7B-Base. Its in-domain ECE is .013 to .015, but it is overconfident on unfamiliar schemas, with ECE up to .22. The setting is the one TypeSafe AI announced with Jev \citep{typesafe2026jev}, trained by an unpublished recipe called RLCD, reinforcement learning for calibrated decisions. Our decision head is an independent re-implementation of the concept; jevmark is the name of its code repository and released adapters.

\textbf{Why logged bandit feedback is the realistic case.} After deployment on a new domain, labels are expensive and rare. What a system does record is whether the action it took turned out right: a ticket reopened or not, a routing corrected or not. This is logged bandit feedback: one outcome per interaction, only for the chosen action, under a known logging policy. The common practice is to fine-tune on the interactions that went well and discard the rest. That discards the negatives, which makes it positive-unlabeled learning, and, as we show, leaves the model overconfident (Section~\ref{sec:4.3}).

\textbf{This paper.} It makes three claims, the third of which is the core.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A corollary on detached proper-score rewards.} The general principle is that a detached, parameter-dependent reward drops the pathwise term of the gradient \citep{schulman2015scg,mohamed2020montecarlo}. Our contribution is the specific algebra when the reward is a proper score of the sampled action's own probability: a wrong action's reward is never below gold's, the lowest-probability wrong action sampled gets the largest advantage, and the signal is exactly zero at $K = 2$ (Section~\ref{sec:3}); and the measured collapse (Section~\ref{sec:4.2}). The concurrent OpenJev-RLCD \citep{gao2026openjev} reaches the same recipe, pathwise gradient only and the score-function term harmful, by a gradient-norm argument for a different object: its REINFORCE term acts on a sampled rationale, not on the answer whose probability is scored. \citet{bereket2025uncalibrated} and \citet{damani2025rlcr} show that REINFORCE with a proper-score reward works when the forecast is a separately emitted probability, and \citeauthor{bereket2025uncalibrated} trace GRPO's overconfidence to dividing the advantage by the group standard deviation. Our group baseline subtracts the group mean only, with no standard-deviation normalisation (\texttt{rlcd.normalize\_std: false} in every full-label run, and a batch mean only in the logged-feedback REINFORCE arm), so their failure mode does not apply, and our pathology is specific to scoring the sampling probability itself.
  \item \textbf{A replication, under a stricter protocol, that full labels leave nothing for bandit objectives.} OpenJev-RLCD's direct-scorer ablation reports that proper-score RL never beat cross-entropy on CLINC150 (94.6 against 96.3 accuracy) and that RLVR collapsed to the mode. We find the same direction and add the transfer-to-unseen-schema protocol, the continued cross-entropy control, four bandit arms under one temperature, and the finding that a single temperature is the strongest cheap fix (Section~\ref{sec:4.2}). The direction is not new.
  \item \textbf{Learning from logged bandit feedback with CAPS.} CAPS is the direct method of logged bandit feedback \citep{dudik2011doubly}, trained with a proper score on the chosen action, the regression-oracle objective \citep{foster2018regcb,foster2020squarecb}. What is new is the package for LoRA-adapted LLM decision heads at 2000 interactions:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item positive-only fine-tuning diagnosed as positive-unlabeled learning \citep{elkan2008pu} and beaten from 2000 interactions on Banking77 and from 500 on emotion, with better calibration and selective risk at every log size;
    \item no gain from propensity weights, the expected behaviour of a well-specified regression \citep{shimodaira2000covariate,byrd2019importance};
    \item calibration to the noisy channel without correction, and backward correction \citep{natarajan2013noisy,patrini2017losscorrection} with its $1/(1-2f)$ factor \citep{angluin1988noisy};
    \item memorisation of a fixed noisy log, and checkpoint selection on the held-out log-likelihood of logged outcomes, which needs no label.
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{What the paper does not show.} The feedback is simulated from gold labels, cleanly or with symmetric flips at a known rate, on two adaptation domains, Banking77 and emotion, with one log each, and at 1.7B CAPS has three training seeds and the baselines one. There is no counterfactual-risk policy learner (POEM or BanditNet), no doubly robust arm and no off-policy value estimate of the learned policy (Section~\ref{sec:5}). The gap to full labels does not shrink as the log grows.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:2}

\textbf{Calibrated decision heads for agents.} A small model that answers typed questions about a state in one forward pass, with a confidence its caller thresholds, is the setting of Jev \citep{typesafe2026jev} and of the other published reconstruction, OpenJev-RLCD \citep{gao2026openjev}. Its use is the cascade: a cheap model answers what it is sure of and defers the rest to a larger model or a person \citep{chen2023frugalgpt,ong2024routellm,belcak2025slm}, and routing work that trains the confidence into the small model finds that calibrated confidence gives the better deferral trade-off \citep{chuang2025selfref,hao2026cascading}. Thresholding the top posterior is selective classification \citep{chow1970reject,geifman2017selective}, and we evaluate the caller by its risk-coverage curve. Reading option-letter logits in one forward pass is the multiple-choice convention \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu,robinson2023mcq,kadavath2022know}, with known selection bias toward option letters and sensitivity to option order \citep{zheng2024selectors,pezeshkpour2024order,zhao2021calibrate}; we randomise option order in every split and report accuracy by gold letter position (R1, Section~3). Language models can also state a confidence in words \citep{lin2022teaching,tian2023justask,xiong2024uncertainty}; our head never generates text, and its confidence is the distribution over the options it read.

\textbf{Learning from logged bandit feedback.} CAPS is the direct method of logged bandit feedback \citep{dudik2011doubly}, the regression-oracle objective \citep{foster2018regcb,foster2020squarecb}, which the bake-off of \citet{bietti2021bakeoff} found competitive without propensity weights. The other family, counterfactual risk minimisation, weights the policy objective by inverse propensities, with variance penalties, self-normalisation and clipping \citep{swaminathan2015crm,swaminathan2015selfnormalized,joachims2018banditnet,bottou2013counterfactual}; doubly robust estimators combine the two \citep{dudik2011doubly}. Simulating bandit feedback from a labelled multiclass dataset is the field's standard protocol \citep{beygelzimer2009offset,dudik2011doubly}. OpenJev-RLCD's stage 1 scores the whole readout distribution against the one-hot label, with full information; CAPS scores one component, the chosen action's probability against its own outcome, because partial feedback reveals nothing else. Importance weights are unnecessary for a well-specified conditional model \citep{shimodaira2000covariate} and have little effect on deep networks trained to convergence \citep{byrd2019importance}. Fine-tuning on confirmed answers only is positive-unlabeled learning, which recovers the posterior only up to a constant \citep{elkan2008pu}, and is the decision-head form of self-training on filtered successes \citep{zelikman2022star,yuan2023rft,gulcehre2023rest}; unlikelihood training is its counterpart that keeps the negatives \citep{welleck2020unlikelihood}. We add the instantiation for LoRA-adapted LLM decision heads at 2000 interactions, the comparison with positive-only fine-tuning, the noisy channel and label-free checkpoint selection.

\textbf{Proper scoring rules as losses and as rewards.} A strictly proper score is minimised in expectation by the true probability \citep{brier1950verification,gneiting2007proper}, which makes it a calibration-respecting loss and, when the forecast is a separately emitted probability, a sound reward. As losses, the Brier and log scores train calibrated networks \citep{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles}, and square loss matches cross-entropy for classification \citep{hui2021square}; the two trade within one family through the reliability and resolution decomposition \citep{murphy1973partition,brocker2009decomposition}. Calibration is summarised by binned ECE \citep{naeini2015ece}, whose bias is why we report Brier and NLL beside it \citep{nixon2019measuring,kumar2019verified,ferrer2024posteriors}, and repaired post hoc by one temperature \citep{guo2017calibration}. As RL rewards on a verbalized or token-level confidence, proper scores yield calibrated models \citep{band2024linguistic,baniharouni2025doubt,damani2025rlcr}, and \citet{bereket2025uncalibrated} show that the estimator, not the score, then decides calibration: an unbiased group baseline gives calibrated forecasts and GRPO's standard-deviation normalisation drives them to the extremes. Our group baseline is the mean only (Section~\ref{sec:3}), so that failure does not arise here.

\textbf{Calibration in RL for language models and the gradient question.} That a detached, parameter-dependent reward drops the pathwise term of the gradient is the general rule of stochastic computation graphs \citep{schulman2015scg,mohamed2020montecarlo}, and our first result is its corollary for a proper score of the sampled action's own probability. REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce} with a group baseline is the estimator of GRPO \citep{shao2024deepseekmath} and, leave-one-out, of RLOO \citep{ahmadian2024rloo}. Outcome-only rewards degrade calibration \citep{openai2023gpt4,kalai2025hallucinate,damani2025rlcr}, and routing the calibration signal through a separate path repairs it \citep{ma2026dcpo}; error-penalised RL with an abstention gate can saturate both the reward gradient and the KL anchor \citep{che2026abstention}. OpenJev-RLCD \citep{gao2026openjev} optimises the readout distribution by the pathwise gradient of a proper score, which it calls the deterministic policy gradient with respect to the reported distribution, measures the score-function term at 7 to 12 times the pathwise term's gradient norm and finds more of it monotonically worse; that term acts on a sampled rationale. Its direct-scorer ablation reports that proper-score RL never beat cross-entropy on CLINC150 (94.6 against 96.3) and that RLVR collapsed to the mode. Our second result agrees under a transfer protocol with a continued cross-entropy control.

\textbf{Noisy feedback.} Feedback flipped at a known rate $f$ is random classification noise on the outcome \citep{angluin1988noisy}, and the observed outcome rate $f + (1-2f)\,P(\mathrm{correct})$ that CAPS fits is the symmetric case of the class-conditional identity \citep{natarajan2013noisy}, with the clean posterior an affine transform of the corrupted one \citep{menon2015corrupted}. Inverting it is backward correction with a known transition matrix \citep{patrini2017losscorrection}, whose error grows by the $1/(1-2f)$ factor of \citeauthor{angluin1988noisy}; the same correction appears for noisy preferences \citep{chowdhury2024robustdpo}. \citet{agarwal2021noisybandit} study flipped bandit feedback with a known rate for an online linear learner. Networks memorise noisy labels given enough passes \citep{zhang2017rethinking,arpit2017memorization}, and early stopping is the provable remedy \citep{li2020earlystopping}. We add that a pathwise proper score on noisy chosen-action feedback learns the noisy posterior without any correction, which is what makes backward correction possible, that the residual is the variance of one draw per interaction, and that early stopping can be driven by the held-out log score of logged outcomes.

\textbf{Calibration under shift and after fine-tuning.} A single temperature is strong in-domain and fragile under shift \citep{ovadia2019shift,desai2020calibration,chen2023closelook}, and our second result says the bandit alternatives are no less fragile on unseen label schemas. Domain-dependent temperatures address the shift directly \citep{yu2022multidomain}. Post-training degrades calibration \citep{openai2023gpt4,zhu2023alignment,leng2025taming}, while a small amount of fine-tuning on graded examples can teach calibration that transfers \citep{kapoor2024taught}. Adaptation costs in-domain accuracy, as LoRA forgets less than full fine-tuning but still forgets \citep{biderman2024lora,kumar2022distort}.

\textbf{Dataset quality and evaluation hygiene.} Our leak probes are leakage detection by exploratory models on non-target features \citep{kaufman2012leakage} against a permutation null \citep{ojala2010permutation} and a control task \citep{hewitt2019control}, the guard the annotation-artifact and shortcut literature calls for \citep{gururangan2018artifacts,poliak2018hypothesis,geirhos2020shortcut}; the duplicate check addresses train-test overlap \citep{elangovan2021leakage,lewis2021overlap}. Benchmark test sets contain label errors \citep{northcutt2021pervasive,northcutt2021confident}. For Banking77, \citet{ying2022banking77errors} flag about 1,400 of 13,083 utterances, about 11 percent, in line with our audit's .12 and .14. The emotion dataset is distantly supervised from hashtags \citep{saravia2018carer}, which human validation puts at about 90 percent label accuracy \citep{abdulmageed2017emonet}; our audit finds a problem in a third of a uniform sample.

\section{Method: Chosen-Action Proper Scoring}\label{sec:3}

\textbf{Setting.} A logged interaction is $(x, O, a, q(a \mid x), r)$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $x$ is a state and $O$ its $K$ options;
  \item $a$ is the action the logging policy $q$ chose, and $q(a \mid x)$ its logging propensity, recorded exactly;
  \item $r \in \{0, 1\}$ records whether $a$ was right.
\end{itemize}

The learner starts from a general decision head $p_\theta$ and sees only the log. In our experiments $q = 0.9\, p_0 + 0.1/K$, epsilon-greedy over the starting model $p_0$, so every option has support.

\textbf{Objective.} CAPS minimises
\begin{equation}
  L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_x\, \mathbb{E}_{a \sim q(\cdot \mid x)} \bigl[ S\bigl(p_\theta(a \mid x), r(x, a)\bigr) \bigr]
\end{equation}
with $S$ a strictly proper binary score: Brier, $(r - p)^2$, by default, or the log score. The empirical mean over the log estimates $L$ directly. The gradient is the pathwise gradient through $p_a$; for Brier it is $-2 (r - p_a)\, \partial p_a / \partial \theta$. A right action is pushed up and a wrong one down.

\textbf{Identity.} CAPS is the direct method of \citet{dudik2011doubly}, with a proper score as the regression loss on the chosen action's outcome and the argmax of the fitted head as the policy, and it is OpenJev-RLCD's stage 1 specialised to partial feedback: where stage 1 scores the whole readout distribution against the one-hot label, CAPS scores the one component the log reveals, the chosen action's probability against its own outcome.

\textbf{Why no propensity weight.} For each $(x, a)$ with $q(a \mid x) > 0$, the inner term is minimised at $p(a \mid x) = P(r = 1 \mid x, a)$, whatever $q$ is. The regression target does not depend on the logging policy as long as it has support, so the unweighted fit is consistent for a well-specified model \citep{shimodaira2000covariate}. The propensity only sets how a finite model trades errors between actions, and $L$ makes that trade as the deployed policy acts. A weight $1/q$ would retarget it to a uniform average over options.

Accuracy is a consequence, not the objective: moving right actions up and wrong ones down moves the argmax. A negative on an option other than the logger's top choice arises only from the logger's randomness. On our main log, 1514 of 2593 negatives (.584) fall on such options, and about .545 of those come from the uniform exploration term. Options the logger never takes are unconstrained by the objective.

\textbf{Corollary (a proper score of the sampled action's probability as a detached reward).} Take $K$ options, gold $g$, and a proper score used as the reward of the sampled action:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Brier: $R(a) = 1 - (r_a - p_a)^2$;
  \item log score: $R(a) = r_a \log p_a + (1 - r_a) \log(1 - p_a)$.
\end{itemize}

The gradient of the expected reward, $\sum_a p_a R(a)$, has a score-function term, $\sum_a R(a)\, \nabla p_a$, and a pathwise term, $\sum_a p_a \nabla R(a)$. Detaching $R$ keeps only the first \citep{schulman2015scg}. Form REINFORCE advantages against the mean of a sampled group, the sample itself included and not divided by the group standard deviation.

(i) If $K > 2$, every wrong action's reward is at least gold's. In each group, the lowest-probability wrong action sampled has the largest reward, so its advantage is non-negative. Whenever the group also samples gold, gold's advantage is non-positive.

(ii) If $K = 2$, all rewards in every group are equal, and the gradient is zero.

\begin{proof}
A wrong action $w$ has $r_w = 0$ and $p_w \le 1 - p_g$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Under Brier, $R(w) = 1 - p_w^2$ and $R(g) = 1 - (1 - p_g)^2$. Under the log score, $R(w) = \log(1 - p_w)$ and $R(g) = \log p_g$.
  \item Both rewards decrease in $p_w$ and equal $R(g)$ at $p_w = 1 - p_g$. Hence $R(w) \ge R(g)$, and the smallest sampled $p_w$ gives the group maximum.
  \item At $K = 2$, $p_w = 1 - p_g$ exactly, so $R(w) = R(g)$. \qedhere
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

The score grades $p_a$ as a forecast of $r_a$, and a low probability on a wrong option is always a good forecast. CAPS uses the same score as a loss on $p_a$ by its pathwise gradient, not as a reward on $a$. Neither statement depends on the standard-deviation normalisation that \citet{bereket2025uncalibrated} identify as GRPO's source of overconfidence: our full-label runs use a group of 4 and subtract its mean only (\texttt{rlcd.normalize\_std: false} in \texttt{configs/rlcd\_06b.yaml} and in the \texttt{config.yaml} of all 31 full-label runs), and the logged-feedback REINFORCE arm subtracts the batch mean only. Nor does the corollary contradict the proper-score rewards of \citet{damani2025rlcr} and \citet{bereket2025uncalibrated}, which score a confidence the model emits separately, not the probability with which it sampled the action being rewarded.

\textbf{Selection.} Train on the first $0.9N$ interactions. Keep the checkpoint, from a 50-step grid, with the highest mean Bernoulli log-likelihood of the logged outcomes under $p(\text{logged action})$ on the last $0.1N$: early stopping on the held-out log score of logged outcomes. No gold label is read. Under noisy feedback this is what prevents memorisation (Section~\ref{sec:4.3}).

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:4}

\subsection{Setup}\label{sec:4.1}

\textbf{Model and data.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Qwen3-0.6B-Base and 1.7B-Base with LoRA (r 16, alpha 32, attention projections) \citep{hu2022lora,qwen2025qwen3}. Answers are read from the letter logits at each question's answer slot in one forward pass.
  \item Supervised training uses CLINC150 intents \citep{larson2019clinc} and SST-5 sentiment \citep{socher2013sst}. Options are sampled per record, with negated yes-or-no phrasings.
  \item Evaluation covers 20 held-out intents and four schemas never trained on: AG News, Yelp \citep{zhang2015charcnn}, emotion \citep{saravia2018carer} and Banking77 \citep{casanueva2020banking77}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Leak and duplicate gates.} Before any GPU run, the data must pass two CPU checks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item state-free leak probes: classifiers on phrasing, question text and structure, which must not beat chance by more than a fixed margin and a shuffled-target null;
  \item train-test duplicate rules.
\end{itemize}

The Banking77 log domain drops 30 messages that match the test split and 31 repeats.

\textbf{Adaptation protocol.} Supervised-to-bandit conversion with a decision head as the logger.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The 0.6B model logs every message of the Banking77 train split once: 9942 messages, each a 10-option choice with gold always present. It samples from $q = 0.9\,p + 0.1/K$ and records only the correctness of its action. Policy accuracy is .836 and the outcome rate .739.
  \item Learners see the first $N \in \{500, 2000, 5000\}$ interactions. All trained learners take the same number of steps, $\max(200, \lceil 10 \times 0.9 N / 32 \rceil)$, at lr 5e-5.
  \item Evaluation is on the full Banking77 test split (3080 records), plus \texttt{test\_indomain} and \texttt{test\_unseen\_intents} for forgetting.
  \item Intervals are 95 percent bootstrap over records \citep{efron1979bootstrap,efron1993introduction}, 1000 resamples, paired across runs.
  \item At N 5000, CAPS-Brier, CAPS-log and \texttt{full\_sft} each have three training seeds, and CAPS-Brier also has a second log draw.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Learners.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Temperature} fitted to the logged outcomes: post-hoc and global, and it changes no argmax.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{positive\_sft}}: cross-entropy on confirmed actions, positive-unlabeled learning with the negatives discarded, not unobserved. What practitioners do.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{full\_sft}}: cross-entropy on gold for every logged interaction. The upper bound, at one label per interaction.
  \item \textbf{Off-policy forms}, all on the same log:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item REINFORCE with reward $r - p_a$, detached, a batch-mean baseline and the importance weight $\min(p/q, 5)$ (\texttt{outcome\_minus\_p\_log});
    \item CAPS with the log score and inverse propensity weighting of the direct-method loss, $\min(1/q, 5)$ divided by its mean over the training prefix (\texttt{ips\_log}), which is importance-weighted regression;
    \item \texttt{positive\_sft} with the same weights (\texttt{ips\_positive}).
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

The weighted forms reweight the direct-method loss. They are not counterfactual risk minimisation, which weights the policy objective itself; that arm was not run (Section~\ref{sec:5}). In tables, CAPS with the Brier score is the run name \texttt{direct\_brier}, and with the log score \texttt{direct\_log}.

\textbf{Metrics.} Accuracy, ECE with 15 equal-width bins, Brier and NLL, and two selective-classification metrics for the caller who thresholds \citep{geifman2017selective}. Questions are ranked by top-1 probability. AURC is the area under the risk-coverage curve, the mean error of the kept answers over all coverages; Cov@eps is the largest share of questions a top-1 threshold keeps with error at most eps, for eps in \{.05, .10, .20\}, with ties in top-1 kept or dropped together. Lower AURC and higher Cov@eps are better. Every trained learner reaches Cov@.20 = 1, and from N 2000 Cov@.10 = 1 as well, so the tables show Cov@.05. The same metrics ranked by the response's confidence field, $1 - H/\ln K$, are stored under \texttt{\_entropy} keys.

A temperature changes the level of the probabilities and only slightly their ranking: one temperature is monotone within a question but can reorder top-1 probabilities across questions. At N 5000 the temperature learner cuts zero-shot ECE from .063 to .020 but moves AURC only from .0333 to .0328 and Cov@.05 from .756 to .765; on CAPS-Brier, whose fitted temperature is near 1, it moves AURC by under .0001.

\subsection{Full labels: the negative results (v2)}\label{sec:4.2}

\textbf{The broken reward.} A first run used Brier as a detached REINFORCE reward on fully labelled in-domain data, group of 4, mean baseline without standard-deviation normalisation.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Score accuracy fell from .636 to .111 on SST-5 and from .505 to .044 on Yelp.
  \item A simulation of the sampler on the SFT model lowered the gold logit in 24.0 percent of questions and raised it in 1.8 percent.
\end{itemize}

This is the corollary of Section~\ref{sec:3} at work.

\textbf{No gain from bandit objectives on full labels.} Five arms:
\begin{itemize}
  \item continued cross-entropy (the control);
  \item two REINFORCE arms with detached rewards, outcome and $r - p$;
  \item CAPS with Brier and with the log score, by the pathwise gradient.
\end{itemize}

Each ran three seeds at 0.6B. Against SFT plus one in-domain temperature (unseen-schema mean ECE .121):
\begin{itemize}
  \item every arm's unseen-schema ECE is higher, by +.031 to +.127;
  \item given its own temperature, the best arm is level, $-$.002 [$-$.006, +.004], and so is the control, +.002 [$-$.001, +.009].
\end{itemize}

When gold is known, whether a sampled answer was right carries no more than the label itself, and one temperature is the strongest cheap fix. OpenJev-RLCD reports the same direction on CLINC150 with a different training setup; the transfer to unseen schemas and the continued cross-entropy control are ours. Figure~\ref{fig:v2_arm_ece} shows the arms.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:5}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulated feedback.} Outcomes come from gold labels, revealed cleanly or flipped symmetrically at a known rate. Real feedback is delayed, biased towards some errors, and of unknown noise rate, and backward correction needs that rate. The learned compression towards the channel is global: in-domain ECE rises by .050 for the noisy run.
  \item \textbf{Domains and sizes.}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Two adaptation domains with one log each; on emotion, \texttt{positive\_sft} and \texttt{full\_sft} have one seed.
    \item One logging policy per domain at epsilon 0.1: .836 accurate on Banking77, .542 on emotion.
    \item Two sizes; at 1.7B, CAPS-Brier has three training seeds and \texttt{positive\_sft} and \texttt{full\_sft} one each, so the 1.7B gaps carry no baseline seed variance.
    \item The noise sweep has one seed per rate.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Missing off-policy arms.} The propensity-weighted arms reweight the direct-method loss; there is no counterfactual-risk policy learner (POEM or BanditNet; \citealp{swaminathan2015crm,joachims2018banditnet}), no doubly robust arm \citep{dudik2011doubly}, and no self-normalised off-policy estimate of the learned policy's value from the log. The no-gain result is shown against reweighted regression, not against the methods built around the weights.
  \item \textbf{Label noise, judged by two language models, no human.}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Banking77} (\texttt{docs/audit/SUMMARY.md}): some label problem in .12 and .14 of a uniform sample, and a clearly wrong label in .04. Of 100 questions both \texttt{full\_sft} and CAPS-Brier miss, the judges find a problem in .66 and .62. The ceiling is plausibly near .96.
    \item \emph{Emotion} (\texttt{docs/audit/EMOTION\_SUMMARY.md}): a problem in .32 and .40 of a uniform sample, mostly a labelling convention that follows emotion words (.20 for both judges).
    \item \emph{Both:} the clean feedback inherits these labels.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Selection.} Checkpoints every 50 steps. At N 2000 the log-score arms peaked at the first candidate, so a finer grid could favour them. At N 500, 50 interactions select.
  \item \textbf{Coverage of the claim.} The gap to full labels does not shrink with N. The claim is that using failures beats discarding them, not that it replaces labels.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:6}

A decision head that can only see whether its chosen action was right should be fitted as the direct method with a proper score: the chosen action's probability against its own logged outcome, by the pathwise gradient. That is CAPS, OpenJev-RLCD's stage 1 restricted to the one component partial feedback reveals. With 2000 interactions it beats positive-only fine-tuning, which is positive-unlabeled learning with the negatives discarded, in accuracy, calibration and selective risk on both domains (Sections~\ref{sec:4.3} and~\ref{sec:4.4}), and on Banking77 it needs no propensity weights (Section~\ref{sec:4.3}); under feedback flipped at a known rate it learns the noisy posterior, backward correction recovers most of the clean calibration, and early stopping on the held-out log score of logged outcomes stops it before it memorises the single noisy draw (Section~\ref{sec:4.3}). CAPS stays below full-label training on both domains (Sections~\ref{sec:4.3} and~\ref{sec:4.4}), though on Banking77 the gap shrinks from 1.5 points at 0.6B to .6 at 1.7B (against one full-label seed), so failures are worth keeping, not a substitute for labels.

The two negative results frame it. Scoring the sampled action's own probability as a detached REINFORCE reward is a corollary of the general rule that detaching a parameter-dependent reward drops the pathwise term, and it rewards wrong actions (Sections~\ref{sec:3} and~\ref{sec:4.2}); this is distinct from proper-score rewards on a separately emitted confidence, and from the standard-deviation normalisation of GRPO, which our mean-only baseline does not use. With full labels, consistent with OpenJev-RLCD, no bandit objective beats cross-entropy plus one temperature, here under transfer to unseen schemas (Section~\ref{sec:4.2}).
